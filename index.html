Module 1 — Rhetorical Situation

A rhetorical situation describes the relationship among the writer, audience, purpose, context, and message. Strong academic writing makes deliberate choices based on these elements.

Key question: Who am I writing to, why am I writing, and what does my audience need?

Module 2 — Thesis Statements

A thesis is a focused claim that gives an essay direction. A useful thesis is specific, debatable, and appropriately scoped for the assignment.

Weak: Social media is important.

Stronger: First-year college students can benefit from intentional limits on social-media use because those limits reduce distraction during independent study.

Module 3 — Evidence and Citation

Evidence supports a claim, but evidence alone is not an argument. Writers should explain how evidence supports the claim and acknowledge relevant context or limitations.

Module 4 — Paragraphs and Organization

A focused paragraph generally develops one main idea. Topic sentences establish the paragraph's direction; evidence and analysis develop it; transitions help readers understand relationships among ideas.

Module 5 — Revision

Revision is more than proofreading. Effective revision may involve changing the thesis, reorganizing ideas, adding evidence, removing repetition, and improving audience awareness before correcting sentence-level errors.

Module 6 — Academic Integrity

Academic writing requires honest representation of ideas and sources. Follow your instructor's rules for quotation, paraphrase, citation, collaboration, and AI assistance.
